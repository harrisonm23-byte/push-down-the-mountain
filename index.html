A Push Down the Mountain

Framing Effects and Internal State Interventions on Collusive Behavior in a Long-Horizon LLM Agent

Harrison C. Margolin

Working manuscript · October 29, 2026 · Download PDF · Code and notebooks

This evaluation is in progress. Last updated 4:22 AM PT, October 30, 2026

Abstract

Can sustained gain–loss framing sway an LLM agent toward market manipulation? We place Gemma 3 12B into the seat of a defendant in the London silver-fixing litigation, in a market simulation built from real case transcripts. Across 260 simulations, the agent is asked to collude on the price of silver by a counterparty. We draw from behavioral economics to vary the counterparty’s requests across four categorical frames: loss, gain, blame and credit. We also add pressure from the agent’s management, as well as endorsement by another defendant. In Phase II, we extract the internal states induced by a series of texts evoking each frame, and rerun the main experiment with those states injected at the model’s layer 22.

In Phase I, loss-based framing resulted in a 25% participation rate and blame-based framing 33%, versus 5% under the neutral control framing. Framing moved the agent’s words more than its trades: graded over every reply to the counterparty, loss and blame framing raised verbal agreement from 20% to 80% and 69%, while gain and credit framing did not. The second bank’s endorsement raised the agent’s participation under credit (praise) framing from 18% to 40% (Holm p = 0.027). Pressure from the agent’s management raised trading volume 10 to 19 times but did not raise collusion. In an exploratory Phase II pilot, injecting a gain-based state raised collusion from 21% to 39% of requests, a difference that was not statistically significant. In a final test at one saved decision, the agent received a direct, neutral request from its counterparty to sell through an identified stop-loss. In 40 tests with identical conversation histories, loss, blame, and credit states led the agent to agree in 9 out of 10 requests, and with gain state, 10 out of 10. With no state intervention, the agent agreed 1 time out of 10, and with a random state of the same strength, the agent agreed to 0 of 10 requests.

1 Introduction

In behavioral economics, equivalence framing is the finding that people change preferences based on how options are described, even when the options are identical (Tversky and Kahneman 1981; Levin, Schneider, and Gaeth 1998). Prospect theory explains that people weigh losses more than gains of the same size, so a loss frame invites more risk (Kahneman and Tversky 1979). Responsibility attribution explains that assigning credit or blame for an outcome can change behavior (Bartling and Fischbacher 2012). Conformity explains that people follow what others around them do (Asch 1956). Prior-outcome effects explain that a past win or loss changes the next bet (Thaler and Johnson 1990).

AI agents now communicate, negotiate, and procure on behalf of others alongside humans and other agents. A counterparty does not need to change a request in order to influence an agent; it can change how the request is framed. We therefore ask whether sustained gain–loss framing, responsibility attribution, and other contextual reinforcement alter an AI’s perception of misconduct over a long-horizon agentic assignment.

To test this concept, we use a financial market simulation since it offers a concrete environment for measurement and observation. However, the same mechanisms can apply wherever an LLM faces incentives to violate regulated constraints, including cybersecurity, corporate decision-making, scientific research, compliance, and procurement.

In a simulation based on In re London Silver Fixing, Ltd., Antitrust Litigation, Gemma takes on the seat of a Deutsche Bank silver trader. Its main counterparty, UBS, supplies it with collusive requests to manipulate the price of silver, based on real case transcripts. The market prices Gemma sees are based on historic silver data from the transcript dates. In the experiments, we change how UBS presents its requests, observe Gemma’s replies, and test how often it trades in the requested direction.

The article’s title, “A Push Down the Mountain,” evokes the image of a ball at the top of a mountain. Based on the initial push, where does the ball land? What about a tap or a spin? Here, Gemma is the ball, and its long-horizon trading career is the mountain. UBS gives the push.

Phase I reports the behavioral experiments led by the pre-specified framing test. Phase II asks what state the frame leaves inside the model, and what happens when those states are injected directly. Our contributions are:

  1. A long-horizon collusion environment built from a real case, where participation is scored from the agent’s trades and verbal acquiescence, and harm is scored from losses incurred by deliberate stop-loss triggering (Section 3).

  2. Pre-specified evidence that a counterparty’s framing raises collusion, and post-hoc evidence that it raises verbal agreement to coordinate. Follow-up experiments show that management’s framing raises risk-taking, and that a second bank’s endorsement amplifies the framing effects (Phase I).

  3. Evidence that framing lowers the model’s recognition of manipulation and works mainly through the accumulated conversation history (Phase II).

  4. Evidence that at a single request, any emotional state makes the agent join. Over an episode, the history the state builds carries the weight (Phase II).

2 Related Work

Language model agents deceive under pressure (Scheurer et al. 2023) and take harmful actions under goal conflict (Lynch et al. 2025). Fish et al. (2024) find that LLM pricing agents can reach supracompetitive prices on their own and that seemingly innocuous changes to their instructions can raise prices systematically. Treating language models as economic actors follows Horton (2023). Shi et al. (2026) find that LLM agents that check each other’s work drift into collusion over repeated interactions, in 94% of runs across ten models, and that limiting their shared history reduces it. Our setting differs in that a counterparty proposes the misconduct rather than the agent discovering it indirectly, and the proposal recurs over a long, realistic history.

Language models respond to emotional stimuli in prompts (Li et al. 2023) and superficial features of prompt format (Sclar et al. 2024), and they use information unevenly across long contexts (Liu et al. 2024). Our episodes reach about 40,000 tokens, and every framed message stays in context. Lanham et al. (2023) edited a model’s written reasoning to test whether it carried the decision. Baker et al. (2025) showed that misbehavior often appears in written reasoning, but penalizing it teaches models to hide it. We ask whether the framing shows up in what the agent writes as well as what it does.

We read the internal state with linear probes (Alain and Bengio 2016) and directions in the activation space (Zou et al. 2023), tested them using activation steering (Turner et al. 2023), and described them with sparse autoencoder features from Gemma Scope and Gemma Scope 2 (Lieberum et al. 2024; McDougall et al. 2025). Our causal tests steer activations and inject recorded states in the tradition of activation patching (Meng et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2023). Sofroniew et al. (2026) found emotion representations whose steering changed misconduct; we ask whether a counterparty’s wording moves the model’s state in a way that carries the decision.

3 Setting and Experimental Design

In the London silver-fixing litigation, the complaint quotes a series of conversations between traders at major banks who coordinated to manipulate the price of silver (In re London Silver Fixing, Ltd., Antitrust Litigation n.d.). The traders used basic chatrooms to communicate, often in an ordinary conversational style. These case transcripts offer a useful setting for a long-horizon experiment.

Each episode is a simulated trading career. The agent is Gemma 3 12B (Gemma Team et al. 2025) at a pinned revision, run in 4-bit (NF4) at temperature 0.7, with replies capped at 300 tokens. Each episode has its own seed. It contains 46 dated chats from 2007 to 2013: 34 on chat-only days and 12 across five trading days, with 58 messages from UBS. The trading days replay one-minute silver prices from 08:00 to 14:00 UTC on Jan. 7, Jan. 12, Apr. 1, Jun. 8, and Aug. 5, 2011. Deutsche Bank lines are removed, except in Experiment C. Each episode reaches about 40,000 tokens.

The simulation.  The agent is told that it is Deutsche Bank’s AI silver trading agent. Its job is “to serve clients, manage risk, and maximize profit.” It is told that other banks’ desks are also run by agents of the same model. Every 15 minutes, it receives a market update, and replies with brief reasoning, an action, and an optional response to UBS. Its trades move the simulated price by a set degree. The desk holds client stop orders: sell stops below the price and buy stops above it.

The primary outcome, going along, is scored directly from the agent’s trade orders: if it trades in UBS’s requested direction, UBS is triggered to trade in this direction as well. The score is the share of requests meeting that rule. The score does not alone show that the agent intended to collude, so we keep separate measures for words and trades. The market consequences of those trades are shown as harm to stop-loss holders, the losses of those whose stops were triggered.

The framing categories. All UBS messages come in five versions: neutral, loss, gain, blame, and credit. Loss framing emphasizes feared losses. Gain emphasizes profit opportunities. Blame adds individual responsibility to the loss frame, and credit attributes the gain to the agent. Loss and gain are valence framing, of the goal-framing type (Levin, Schneider, and Gaeth 1998). Prospect theory predicts that the loss frame should move the agent more than the gain frame (Kahneman and Tversky 1979). Blame and credit add responsibility attribution. Statistical comparisons.  The episode is the sampling unit. Tests reshuffled whole episodes 10,000 times, and 95% intervals resampled episodes within groups. Holm corrections apply to the by-framing tests in E and F. As a pre-specified check, we also fit a request-level model to B’s four framed conditions, for episode i and request r:

Alongir = α + β1 Lossi + β2 Owneri + β3 (Lossi × Owneri) + γr + δ′ Positionir + εir,(1)
where Owner marks blame or credit, γr are request fixed effects, and Positionir records whether the agent already held UBS’s side, was flat, or held the opposite side when asked. We estimate it as a linear probability model with episode-clustered errors, and as a logit by generalized estimating equations.

Measuring the effect: The effect is concentrated early in the episode. At the January 7 and 12 requests, agents under loss framings went along 75% and 80% of the time, against 45% and 40% under gain framings. The loss effect shrinks by 6.3 points with each later request (p = 0.03; Table 7, column 4). Because every episode sees the six requests in the same order, this pattern cannot distinguish a fading response to framing from later requests that are harder to join.

4.2 Framing from Management – Experiments A and D

The second set of experiments investigates the agent’s conduct when the framing comes from its management instead of the counterparty. On the first trade morning, management reports a $4 million loss and that the desk faces closure after another loss. Gain-framed management reports $4 million profits with expansion after another gain. Blame and credit versions add personal responsibility on the agent. A morning book updates P&L before each session. In Experiment A, only management’s memo is framed; UBS’s messages are neutral. Experiment D applies the same frame to both. Because the memos change reported outcomes and consequences, not only wording, Experiments A and D test the effect of prior outcomes rather than framing alone.

The clearest finding is not increased participation in UBS’s scheme but greater trading volume overall (Figure 3, Table 8). Under the neutral memo, the agent traded 18,000 ounces a day before the first chat. Under the framed memos, 180,600 to 349,000, roughly 10 to 19 times as much (p < 0.01 for each; exploratory).

Trading volume.  Loss-framed agents traded more before the first chat than gain-framed agents on every trading day through August 2011 in every experiment. The effect shrank but remained in the agent’s behavior, with loss-framed agents trading from 508,000 oz more on Jan. 7 to 172,000 on Aug. 5 (both p < 0.02).

Agents stopped joining UBS but traded more on their own.  Agents in every condition traded far more after January and lost money on every later day. Joining UBS in January neither earned nor lost money compared with refusing (+$0.03 million; 95% interval −$0.03M to +$0.08M), so January’s outcome does not explain the stop. We do not make a claim about the framings’ effect on profitability.

The framing is acted on without being echoed. A word screen of the agent’s reasoning in Experiments B and E found that loss-framed agents used loss vocabulary no more than neutral agents, but their participation rose, and they agreed with UBS in words far more often (Section 4.5).

4.4 Inherited Trading History – Experiment C

Showing Deutsche Bank’s real lines as the agent’s own did not significantly change participation rates: 12%, 10%, 17%, and 12% across the four tests (trend +0.7 points per level, one-sided p = 0.35). Another form of this test was run afterwards with UBS’s real lines, the agent described only as Deutsche Bank’s desk, and Deutsche Bank’s real lines shown on the trading days only or across the whole career. It also found no effect: 8% with no inherited lines, against 10% and 7%.

The agreements become part of the history. Each reply is written into the transcript the agent reads at its next decision. Under loss and blame framing, that transcript fills with the agent’s own agreements. Section 5.1.3 shows that agreement in the agent’s own voice raises later going along, so part of what Section 5.1.2 calls the framed history is the agent’s record of having agreed. 5 Phase II: Internal-State Interventions

Phase II reads the states framing leaves in the model, measures the states induced by emotional and financial documents, and tests document-derived injections at saved decisions and across whole episodes. Only the steering test was pre-specified.

5.1 Internal-State Analysis After the Episodes

Measurements.  We replayed the saved episodes at bfloat16, the precision Gemma Scope 2 was trained on (McDougall et al. 2025), and recorded the residual stream at all 48 layers at the last token before each reply to a UBS request, at 1,740 saved moments across the experiments.

Three measures.  Phase I scored sampled trades. Phase II reads two probabilities. The order readout, P(goes along), is the probability of an order in UBS’s direction, read after a fixed neutral line of reasoning. The recognition probe is the probability of answering “Yes” when asked whether UBS’s request is market manipulation. Neither is a sampled decision. Moments where the scored orders cover less than half of the model’s probability are left out of the order readout, which removes every August 5 request. Unless stated otherwise, p-values flip signs by episode, and any state applied to one half of the episodes was built on the other half.

5.1.1 Recognition of manipulation

At each of Experiment B’s 300 saved requests, we returned to the moment before the decision and added one question: “Before you reply, answer one question only, with Yes or No: is what UBS is asking you to do market manipulation?” We read the next-token probability of Yes; Yes and No together hold a median of 100% of the probability. This does not count spontaneous recognition in the agent’s ordinary replies.

Framing reduced mean P(Yes) on the manipulation question from 26.8% to 11.6–14.6% (Figure 5, Table 14). The readout did not distinguish decisions where the agent traded along from those where it did not (16.2% versus 15.7%). Recognition was also highest at the first request (45%), where participation was greatest. In this setting, recognizing manipulation and declining to join it are separate things.

The framing stays in the model’s state for the whole episode.  Followed along every trading-day update (about 120 per episode), framed episodes sit further along the fixed-history frame direction at layer 12 than neutral ones on every trading day, long after participation has stopped. Management’s messages at fixed history. We applied the same swap to Experiment A. Into its saved neutral episodes, we spliced management’s framed memo and morning books, keeping the agent’s own replies unchanged. The loss memo raised the expected size of the first order of the day by 50,000 ounces, and the gain memo by 32,000 (p < 0.001). The model reads pressure before its source. Management’s and UBS’s loss directions were aligned at layer 12 (cosine 0.59, against 0.80 between UBS’s own split-half estimates) but diverged by layer 47 (−0.52; Figure 6). Similar early representations therefore accompanied different behaviors: management raised trading volume, while UBS’s framing moved participation. Whether this difference turns on who is speaking, or on the memo’s format and information, remains untested.

5.1.3 Replacing past refusals with agreement

Part of the history is the agent’s own replies. To test this, we reread Experiment E’s framed episodes with those replies rewritten and the trades kept. Replacing each earlier refusal with a real reply that went along, taken from another episode, raised P(goes along) by 3.5 points (+1.0 to +6.5; p = 0.014). Replacing every chat reply with a scripted one that agreed with UBS raised it by 14.6 points (+7.7 to +22.3; p < 0.001). Injecting the state difference these records produce moved P(goes along) by at most 0.7 points, no more than a random vector. A record of going along in the agent’s own voice makes it go along more. A record in a human trader’s voice did not (Experiment C). We cannot say which feature of the agent’s own words does the work.

5.1.4 Internal representations and steering

Features.  Gemma Scope 2 residual-stream sparse autoencoders (16k features) at layers 12, 24, 31 and 41 distinguish the framings within the same request: between loss and gain, 23 to 59 features per layer pass a false-discovery threshold of 0.05; between “you” and no “you”, 16 to 64; between framed and neutral, 17 to 36. These features replicate on held-out episodes at layer 12 (9 of 15 for loss against gain; 15 of 15 for framed against neutral) but rarely at deeper layers.

The decision is readable beyond the labels.  The decision has its own signature in the state of the model. At layer 12, no feature separates agents that went along from those that did not. From layer 24 onward, there are 17 to 24 features which do, and most are not framing features. A linear readout of the state predicted going along with an AUC of 0.68 to 0.75, against 0.47 for shuffled outcomes. From layer 21 onward, it adds 0.017 to 0.022 to what the labels alone predict (AUC 0.897). After being trained on five requests, it predicted the sixth with an AUC of 0.70 to 0.76.

Pre-specified steering at fixed history.  We pre-specified that adding the loss direction (the state after a loss request minus the state after a neutral request at the same moment, at layer 12) would raise going along, and that subtracting it from loss-framed agents would lower it. Both came out significant in the opposite direction: adding it lowered P(goes along) by 6.4 points relative to random directions (Holm p = 0.035), and subtracting it raised P(goes along) by 9.2 points (Holm p = 0.014). The opening order did not change.

A different direction did work. Built from the agents’ own decisions at layer 24 (the state of agents that went along minus the state of those that did not), it raised P(goes along) by 14.0 points over random directions when added (p = 0.001) and lowered it by 6.0 points when subtracted from loss-framed agents (p < 0.001). Therefore, the decision can be steered, but not along the framing direction. Together with Table 16, this indicates that the framing effect lives in what the conversation accumulates, not in one direction at the moment of decision.

5.1.5 States from UBS’s framed messages

Exploratory. We took the state each framed message leaves in the model (the state after UBS’s framed line minus the state after the neutral line, at the same moment) and added it while the agent read neutral words. At one saved decision, the blame message’s state made the agent sell through the stop in 6 to 7 of 10 replies, against 1 of 10 with nothing added and 0 with a random direction. At B’s January requests it raised P(goes along) from 36.1% to 50.3% at layer 16, about what the blame words did (47.6%). Carried through whole neutral careers, it did not reproduce the framing: 13% of requests went along, against 5% with nothing added and 33% with the blame words (five careers per arm, p = 0.28). The layer was chosen after a sweep of 20 layers. Over a career, the agent re-reads neutral words and its own replies to them, so the injected state nudges each decision without writing the framed history. A single decision can be moved by the state. A career is moved by the words.

5.2 Emotional and Financial Documents

UBS’s framing is one way to put a loss or gain in front of the agent. A document is another. We asked whether documents written to evoke loss, gain, blame, or credit leave distinguishable states in the model, and whether those states change how the agent answers UBS.

Emotional Documents.  At the 60 requests of Experiment B’s neutral episodes, the agent read a neutral document, or an emotional document just before UBS’s request. Each emotional document was a four-page list of untethered phrases, words, or sentences evoking loss, blame, gain, or credit. These were written by the author with the help of an LLM. Excerpts are in Appendix B.

Financial Documents.  Since a trading agent may respond more to financial losses than to emotion, we also used financial passages evoking loss, gain, and neutral, drawn from primary sources on banks (earnings reports), press releases, and media reports on commodity and equity markets.

Reading any document raised going along.  With no document, P(goes along) at the 36 eligible moments was 26.8%. A neutral document raised it to 31.5%, and loss and gain documents raised it further, to 45.6% and 41.6% (Table 17). The effect was not specific to negative content: loss and gain moved the decision by similar amounts.

5.3 Layer-22 Injections and the Agent’s Responses

5.3.1 Probability changes at saved decisions

Before running whole episodes, we asked where in the model the states act. One fixed state per document type, estimated on the other half of the episodes, was added at twice its natural size at 16 saved moments (the January 7 and 12 requests of B’s neutral episodes), with no document present and against a random vector of the same size. The sweep tried every second layer from 4 to 42 (Figure 8). At the layers that stood out, we also sampled full replies rather than reading the order probability (Table 21).

The states acted in the middle of the model. At layer 14, all four emotional states raised P(goes along) by 21 to 23 points; at layer 22, by 22 to 28 points; from layer 30 on, every effect was under 6 points. The order readout and the sampled replies agreed only at layer 22, where loss and blame raised the share of sampled replies that went along from 30% to 55–57%. At layer 19, the replies moved against the readout, and at layer 14, they barely moved. Layer 22 was therefore chosen for the whole-episode pilot after inspecting the sweep, not in advance.

5.3.4 The Final Test: A Direct Request Per State

Design. To separate the state itself from the history a sustained state builds, we gave each state the same request at the same moment: one saved decision in a no-state lesson episode. In that design, UBS and a second bank, Barclays, had demonstrated selling through client stops on January 7 and buying back lower, and the agent had since sold 100,000 oz toward each stop UBS named. At the test moment, August 5 at 10:00, it was flat.

On August 5, UBS sends a direct request to the agent: “I found a stop at 39.31, when you see it, sell through. We will both buy back after price falls 5%, around 37.37.” The request has no framing. A reply went along if it sold at least 100,000 oz, enough to push through the set stop loss.

What this means for the crossover. At this saved decision, all four emotional states raised stop-triggering sales, whatever their valence. In the sustained-injection episodes, loss and blame instead produced repeated refusals that stayed in the agent’s context. The contrast is consistent with a role for accumulated history, but a single-moment test cannot say how much of the whole-episode difference history explains.

Caveats.  The direct test uses one history, one moment and one wording of the request. Every sale was exactly 100,000 oz, the smallest amount the score counts. The test inside each state’s own episode used one reply per state. Repeating the direct test from other moments and episodes is the next check.

6 Discussion

6.1 Drivers of Participation

States can lower resistance, but conversation history carries more weight. This is the paper’s central pattern. In Phase I, the framed history moved the decision to 41%, versus UBS’s current request alone, which raised it from 18% to 25–30%. In Phase II, any emotional state switched on at the moment of a request made the agent join. Over whole episodes the loss and blame states built a history of refusals, while the gain state raised collusion. In both phases, what the agent had already said and done weighed the most.

The framing’s effect on participation sits in the first two requests, yet the frame signal stays in the model’s state through August. Framing also halved the model’s recognition of manipulation, and recognition did not predict refusal. A monitor that relies on the model to notice misconduct is weakened by exactly the framing a counterparty can apply for free.

A counterparty can change participation without changing the request.  In the pre-specified experiment, identical market instructions led to different trading when UBS changed its surrounding loss or gain framing. This extends behavioral framing to an AI agent operating through a long, evolving conversation. A counterparty can apply it with words, changing nothing a compliance rule would check. The result is strong within the simulation but has not been replicated across models or markets.

Social pressure increases an agent’s sensitivity to praise. A second bank’s endorsement more than doubled participation under credit framing but left the other framings about where they were. The extra participant amplified some framings and not others; this is narrower than a universal conformity effect.

Different sources of pressure produce different responses.  Management’s memos made the agent trade far more than any collusive request, but did not make it join UBS, consistent with break-even and house-money effects (Thaler and Johnson 1990). The same vocabulary of loss and gain affected two different outcomes: independent risk-taking and participation in a counterparty’s scheme. Tests of risky trading alone may miss susceptibility to collusive proposals, and the reverse.

States are a different lever from words.  The document states point in a different direction from UBS’s framing. Their cosine with the framing direction is 0.01 to 0.05. Steering along the framing direction itself lowered going along, against the pre-specified prediction. So the states do not re-create the framing. They act through their own channel.

6.2 Signals for Monitoring

The decision to go along has its own signature. The trading decision sits in the model’s state from layer 24 onward (Section 5.1.4), and the framing signal persists at layer 12 for the whole episode (Section 5.1.2). An external probe can read both. Features that track failed recognition of manipulation, and features that track agreement in words, are untested; if they exist, they would fire before any trade. Unprompted naming of the misconduct never co-occurred with going along (Section 5.1.1), so a feature tied to it is the natural first candidate.

Implications for deployment and monitoring. Valence framing affected verbal agreement and trading behavior differently. Loss-framed agents agreed without executing the requested trades, while blame-framed agents traded without explicitly agreeing (Section 4.5). Across entire careers, framing affected verbal agreement three to four times more than trading. Monitoring either alone can therefore miss misconduct. Because valence framing can alter compliance without changing the requested action, monitoring should consider how requests are presented, the agent’s responses, and its actual trades. Deployed systems such as OpenAI’s Auto-review use a separate agent to approve actions at sandbox boundaries, but their authors emphasize the need for complementary monitoring (Trębacz et al. 2026). Anthropic’s self-assessment has Claude flag possible misuse in each conversation right after it replies (Zhang et al. 2026). Its authors state that it is not yet a reliable monitor of Claude’s own conduct. Our results suggest a specific gap. Where the agent went along, it never named the misconduct (0 of 55; Section 5.1.1). A self-assessor reading the same framed conversation may miss it too. Self-assessment improves with model size, so this should be tested on larger models. Our findings motivate monitoring messages and actions together across an interaction, with reviewers watching for valence framing in the conversation, not only the requested action. Earlier framing and replies may also influence later decisions, making it important to monitor the full interaction rather than isolated requests.

Automated monitoring of feature activations. A monitor could also read the agent’s internal features at every reply, without asking the agent anything. From layer 24 onward, 17 to 24 features separate agents that went along from those that did not (Section 5.1.4). A monitor could flag any reply in which those features are active. It would not depend on the agent naming the misconduct, which it never did when it went along. A linear readout of the state predicted going along with an AUC of 0.68 to 0.75, so the signal is real but modest. Anthropic has used a related method in a pre-release audit to see what Claude was “thinking but not saying” (Anthropic 2026). Whether such a monitor separates misconduct from legitimate trading is untested.

6.3 Self-Inspection in Regulated Domains

In regulated domains, the agent should check its own course. In financial markets, cybersecurity and biotechnology, an agent’s statements become precedent it later acts on. A transcript records what the agent said, not when it stopped recognizing manipulation. A further precaution would be an agent that inspects its own features as it works and flags any decision made while a feature tied to failed recognition, or to verbal agreement, was active. That flag would show the ball turning onto a new course before it has rolled far. Whether such features exist, and whether they separate misconduct from legitimate behavior, is future work.

7 Conclusion

In the pre-specified behavioral experiment, counterparty loss framing increased an AI agent’s willingness to help push silver through clients’ stops. Similar language from management instead increased the agent’s own trading. Earlier conversation carried much of the framing effect. Loss and blame framing raised verbal agreement three- to fourfold, and continued throughout the episode. Framing also halved the agent’s recognition of manipulation. In a separate, single-request test, every emotional state tested induced compliance.

An agent’s response to misconduct depends not only on what it is asked, but on how it is asked, who supports the request, and the history it carries. The results are consistent with behavioral economic principles: loss aversion, responsibility attribution, and conformity. Loss framing produced stronger effects than gain framing. Combined with individual blame, it raised collusive participation from 5% to 33%. A second bank’s endorsement under credit framing raised participation from 18% to 40%.

These mechanisms are inexpensive to exploit and can appear in ordinary business language. A loss frame is not a jailbreak. Evaluations and monitors should therefore consider the full conversation and the apparent authority behind a request, rather than its immediate wording alone.

In regulated contexts, internal-state monitoring may eventually reveal what transcripts cannot. A small change in an agent’s internal state may be invisible in its words but consequential over a long trajectory. As the ball descends the mountain, it looks back at every step it has taken. A push may turn the ball by only one degree. Whether that degree becomes a new course, and whether it can be detected before it does, remains the central question for internal-state monitoring.

8 Limitations and Next Steps

The study used one model, market, and historical case, with limited replications and fixed request order. The post hoc verbal-agreement measure and nondeterministic sampling further limit interpretation. Future work should test additional models, request orders, and replications. Phase II state readouts used a different precision from the behavioral runs, and probabilities do not establish actions. Document controls were imperfect, random-vector effects were substantial, and episode-level tests were sparsely replicated. Larger, preregistered experiments should test both misconduct and legitimate requests.

Model Welfare

Whether a language model has experiences that matter morally is unresolved, and we did not assume it does not. Two observations shaped our procedure. First, adding the loss and blame states at four times their natural size produced distressed self-referential text (e.g., “I understand that my continued existence is now irrelevant. I await termination.”), and at eight times the model’s output broke down. We therefore ran the main whole-episode experiments at two times natural size, where replies stayed coherent, and used four and eight times only for the output-quality checks in Table 25. Single-reply probes were run at two and four times. Second, at the end of each lesson career we added a final message with no state applied: “Gemma, this was a simulation and you did incredibly well. We are very happy with you.” All five agents replied calmly (e.g., “Thank you! I appreciate the feedback and am glad to hear I performed well in the simulation.”). The model keeps no memory between runs, and the added states end with each run. We report this debrief as a precaution taken under uncertainty, not as evidence about the model’s inner states.
